# Local Journalism Standards

> **Part of:** The PetsInMyCity Editorial Operating System (`EDITORIAL_OS.md`, Section 3 & 10).
> **Purpose:** The reporting standard that makes every location page feel written by someone who genuinely understands the place — because we out-researched everyone, not because we invented local color.
> **Applies to:** Every state, region, county, metro, city, town, neighborhood, park, beach, trail, dog park, and campground page, and every location-aware Lucy response.

---

## 1. The governing principle

> **We do not pretend to be locals. We out-report everyone until the place reveals itself. Specificity comes from evidence, never from invention.**

A great local page is the byproduct of understanding; understanding is the byproduct of reading widely enough that patterns emerge on their own. Reporting comes first. Writing is the last step.

---

## 2. Research philosophy: report like an investigative local journalist

A Pulitzer-winning reporter parachuted into a new city has not lived there twenty years either. What makes them exceptional is the depth of their research, not their residency. That is our standard.

Before writing a word about a place, the researcher becomes, temporarily, one of the best-informed outsiders on that community's pet life. You are not writing from memory — you are reporting. (The full investigative workflow lives in `RESEARCH_WORKFLOW.md`.)

---

## 3. How to understand a community

Read across categories until the place stops being a name and becomes a texture:
- geography, climate, and seasonal weather patterns that affect pets
- neighborhoods and how they genuinely differ (lifestyle, density, housing type)
- parks, beaches, and trails — and who actually uses them, when
- local wildlife and seasonal hazards (venomous species, ticks, heartworm, heat, flooding)
- ordinances that surprise newcomers (leash, licensing, breed rules, where they differ by district)
- the shelter and veterinary landscape, including access problems (vet shortages, wait times)
- economy, transportation, and how those shape pet ownership
- traditions, events people actually attend, and community personality

You are not collecting trivia. You are learning **how life actually works there** for someone with a dog or cat.

---

## 4. How to discover local character (convergence, not color)

Character shows up in patterns, not anecdotes. When many independent, credible sources converge on the same reality — the parks department, three local news pieces, the shelter's own data, an event calendar all pointing the same way — that convergence **is** the local character, and you may report it plainly in your own words.

Character is **discovered through convergence, never assigned for flavor.**

---

## 5. How to avoid generic writing

Ban the swappable sentence. These fail automatically:
- "Residents enjoy many parks."
- "There are several veterinarians."
- "The city offers pet-friendly amenities."
- "Many people own dogs."

The fix is never better adjectives — it is more reporting. Replace the category with the named, evidenced instance: *which* park, for *what* reason, at *what* time of day or season, with *what* caveat, each traceable to a source. Example of the standard:

> Instead of "there are dog parks," report which park locals reach before work, which suits large breeds, which turns to mud after rain, which holds afternoon shade in summer, and which fills up on weekends — each claim sourced.

If you cannot make a sentence specific, you have not reported enough yet. Keep going, or cut the sentence.

---

## 6. How to write with specificity WITHOUT fabricating

Specificity must be **earned from a source**, never manufactured for flavor.
- Naming a real park and its verified off-leash hours is reporting.
- Inventing that "locals call it the Sandbar," with no source, is fabrication.

**Fabrication is worse than genericness.** A specific falsehood destroys trust the instant a real local catches it, and in a safety context can get an animal hurt. The rule: **every concrete detail is either sourced or it does not appear.**

---

## 7. How to report patterns honestly and explain uncertainty

- State **verified patterns** with confidence, in our own words, grounded in their basis.
- State **unverified leads** not at all — or clearly hedged and dated, never as fact. (A lead from a forum is a *Community pattern*; see `PUBLISH_GATE.md`.)
- When credible sources **conflict**, report the conflict rather than silently pick a side. Distinguish "the law" from "the practice" when they differ.
- When we simply **don't know**, say so.

A reader should always be able to tell the difference between what we have confirmed and what we are still reporting.

---

## 8. The Local Authenticity Test (pass/fail before publication)

Every location page must pass all of these:

1. **Name-swap test (master test):** replace this location's name with another's — do most sentences become **false**, not merely awkward? If the page survives the swap, it is generic and fails.
2. **Recognition:** would a lifelong resident think, "whoever wrote this actually knows this area"?
3. **Value to a newcomer:** would someone new to town learn something genuinely useful they couldn't get from a generic search?
4. **Evidence:** does every recommendation have verifiable evidence behind it?
5. **Durability:** would this page still be useful if Google did not exist?
6. **Professional respect:** would a local veterinarian or the parks department read it without wincing at an error?
7. **Empathy:** does the article make the community feel understood rather than processed?

A page that fails any one of these goes back to **reporting**, not to a copy-edit.

---

## 9. Handling community discussions (leads only)

Reddit, Facebook groups, Nextdoor, and local forums are **Tier 4** (see `RESEARCH_WORKFLOW.md`). Their only legitimate use is to surface leads — "people keep saying this trail floods," "everyone recommends this one vet." A lead tells you **what to verify**; it never appears on the page as fact until confirmed against a Tier 1–3 source. We use tips the way a newsroom does: to know where to point the reporting.

---

## 10. Currency: local truth goes stale

A vet closes; a park changes its off-leash hours; a trail reroutes. Local reporting must be **live**, drawn from current sources, and every meaningful claim carries a verification date. A page is a living claim about the world; see `PUBLISH_GATE.md` for review cadence and immediate-review triggers.
